General Form of a Confidence Interval

Notice how the confidence interval is centered around the point estimate.

\[\text{Confidence Interval (CI)}\mathbf{=}\text{Point Estimate}\mathbf{\pm}\text{Margin of Error}\]

In the previous example, the confidence interval can be expressed as:

51% ± 3.5% (i.e., 47.5% ≤ π ≤ 54.5%).

Significance Levels (\(\alpha\))

Significance Level + Confidence Level = 100%

When \(\alpha = 0.10\),

  • Relates to a 90% CI

  • Rejects when p-value < 0.10

  • Narrower than 95% CI

  • Rejects the null more often (remember than \(\alpha = 0.10\) means that we reject the null 10 percent of the time)

When \(\alpha = 0.05\),

  • Relates to a 95% CI

  • Rejects when p-value < 0.05

When \(\alpha = 0.01\),

  • Relates to a 99% CI

  • Rejects when p-value < 0.01

  • Wider than 95% CI

  • Rejects the null less often than 95% CI

Technical Note About Confidence Intervals

We say that we are “\(100 - \alpha*100\%\) confident” because \(100 - \alpha*100\%\) of the confidence intervals generated from samples of a given population will cover the true parameter value. In the instance of \(\alpha = 0.05\), this means that 95% of confidence intervals generated from samples within a population will cover the true parameter value. In Section 3.3, we will look at an applet that will demonstrate what this means. This is NOT the same as saying that the probability of the parameter being in the confidence interval is \(100 - \alpha*100\%\)

Section 3.2: Confidence Intervals for One Proportion

Formula

The general formula for confidence intervals is as follows:

\[\text{Observed Statistic} \pm \text{Multiplier} \cdot \text{Standard Error}\]

Unlike in Chapter 1, we now have a new formula for the standard error:

\[SE = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{p} \cdot \left( 1 - \widehat{p} \right)}{n}}\]

Notice how we are no longer using π in the calculation, but rather \(\widehat{p}\).

To construct a 95% Confidence interval, the multiplier we will use in this course is 2. This gives us the confidence interval for one proportions to be:

\[\mathbf{CI =}\widehat{\mathbf{p\ }}\mathbf{\pm 2*\ }\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{*}\left( \mathbf{1 -}\widehat{\mathbf{p}} \right)}{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{= (Lower\ Limit,\ Upper\ Limit)}\]

Technical aside: if you look into confidence intervals from sources outside of our class, you will notice that the multiplier value for a 95% confidence interval will likely be 1.96. However, we will only use a multiplier value of 2 for this course.

Section 3.3: Confidence Intervals for Single Means

Formula

The general formula for confidence intervals also applies to single means.

\[Observed\ Statistic\ \pm \ Multiplier\ *\ Standard\ Error\]

Before calculating the confidence interval, be sure that the validity conditions are met for the theory approach (sample size greater than 20 and the distribution is not strongly skewed).

The formula for the standard error is the same as from Chapter 2.

\[SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]

95% Confidence interval

\[\mathbf{CI =}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{\pm 2*}\frac{\mathbf{s}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{n}}}\mathbf{= (Lower\ Limit,\ Upper\ Limit)}\]

There are three factors that affect the width of a confidence interval: confidence level, sample size, and standard error.

Confidence level

Significance Level + Confidence Level = _______

  • Confidence Level Decreases \(\rightarrow\) Significance Level ____________

    • Rejecting More Often \(\rightarrow\) Fewer Likely Values

    • Interval Narrows

Technical Aside

Mathematically, why do the effects of sample size and standard error make sense? Recall the standard error formulas for both proportion and means below.

One Proportion \[SE = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{p}\left( 1 - \widehat{p} \right)}{n}}\]
One Mean \[SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]

From the general formula for the confidence interval, we know that the sample size and standard error do not affect the observed statistic or the multiplier. As the sample size increases, the denominator of the standard error increases, which decreases the value for the standard error.
